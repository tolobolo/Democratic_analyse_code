
1. The Creator & NameSystem: Majority Judgment (Le Jugement Majoritaire).   Creators: Michel Balinski and Rida Laraki, two French researchers and mathematicians based in Paris.   2. How the System WorksInstead of picking just one candidate (First-Past-The-Post) or putting candidates in numerical order (Ranked-Choice), voters assign an independent quality grade to every party:$$\text{Excellent} \quad > \quad \text{Very Good} \quad > \quad \text{Good} \quad > \quad \text{Acceptable} \quad > \quad \text{Poor} \quad > \quad \text{Reject}$$Finding the Winner: The system collects all the grades for a party, sorts them, and finds the Median Grade (the exact middle vote at the 50% mark). The party with the highest median wins.   Breaking Ties: If two parties share the same median grade (e.g., both get a median of Good), the system checks who has more votes strictly above or fewer strictly below the median to determine the winner.3. Key Findings from Our DiscussionWhy "Reject" Matters: Reject is the absolute lowest grade. It pulls down polarizing candidates who are loved by some but hated by many, favoring broader consensus candidates who are widely accepted.Does Dirty Voting / "Bullet Voting" Break It?If a group of voters gives their favorite Excellent and everyone else Reject, it does not break the median unless that dirty group makes up over 50% of all voters.If a critical mass of voters plays dirty, the system turns into a basic "us vs. them" headcount, but it still protects moderate options better than traditional single-vote systems.No Split Votes: Two similar parties (e.g., two center-left parties) don't steal votes from each other or hand the win to an opposing party—voters can simply give both parties high grades without penalizing either.Impact on Radical Policies: Because candidates need a high median score across the whole population to win, extreme candidates are forced to soften their stances, leading to smoother, less radical policy changes over time.4. How to Test It in CodeNo "Magic Numbers" Needed: You don't need numerical scores or averages. Grades have a natural sequence (Excellent > Poor > Reject), so a sorted list or an Enum in code handles the evaluation.Testing Reality: To see how the system holds up, test scenarios with different voter groups—such as Honest Voters vs. Strategic Attackers or Polarized Left/Right Voters vs. Centrist Compromise Voters.


5. Code
All code is in election.py, tests in test_election.py (pytest). Grades are the Grade Enum with no numeric values; their order is the declaration order in GRADES_DESC (best first). A ballot is dict[candidate, Grade]; a missing grade counts as Reject (handled in grades_for only). Median: sort a candidate's grades best to worst and take the middle one. Even count: if the two middle grades are equal that is the median, otherwise drop the worst grade and try again. Ties between parties with the same median: the party with more grades above the median wins. If still tied, remove the worst grade from both parties and start over (median, then count above, then remove worst again). Numbers are never used, neither to generate grades nor to evaluate them.

Voter model (no numbers, no hidden logic; real people do not follow a formula):
- Every voter picks one random favourite party (Excellent) and gives every other party a random grade from Very Good down to Reject (random.choice). The favourite is the voter's single vote under plurality and Storting.
- There is no honest/strategic split any more; it was removed because it did not fit the one-vote systems.
- Do not reintroduce latent quality scores, positions or noise. Numbers were removed on purpose.

Districts and mandates: voters are split over the 19 Storting districts (DISTRICTS table, official 2025 seat counts, 169 total, population 1.1.2024 for the voter split). Each district gives up one mandate (seats_in = official - 1, 150 in total); the 19 given up are the "Utjevning" mandates. Every system is counted per district so all three hand out seats:
- Majority Judgment: MJ ranking per district, then split_seats gives the district's mandates to the winner (--split 1, default) or shares them among the top parties by weights (--split 2,1,1 = half/quarter/quarter, --split 3,2,1 = most/ok/least). Largest-remainder rounding, parties beyond the weights get nothing.
- Plurality: same split rule on the per-district vote ranking (one vote per ballot for the favourite, collect_votes called once per district and shared with Storting).
- Storting (Norway): modified Sainte-Lague (sainte_lague, divisors 1.4, 3, 5, 7, ...) per district with that district's seats_in. Not D'Hondt. The split switch never applies here.
Sperregrense switch (--sperregrense / --no-sperregrense, UI radio):
- ON (default): the 19 Utjevning mandates are a national pool counted on national vote totals, only among parties with at least THRESHOLD (4%) of the national vote (eligible; falls back to all parties if none qualify). MJ uses the national MJ ranking + split, plurality the national vote ranking + split, Storting sainte_lague on eligible national votes. This approximates Norway's levelling seats; the real deficit-based levelling calculation is not modelled.
- OFF: Utjevning is a real 20th district with its own simulated voters (same voters-per-mandate rate as the rest) and 19 mandates, counted exactly like any other district by all three systems. No threshold anywhere.
count_all returns an Election with national seats, per-district seats and the largest party per system. The old single-pool helpers (plurality_from_votes, storting_from_votes) remain for tests only.

Run: pytest, then python election.py --voters 10000 --split 2,1,1 --no-sperregrense (default parties: Far-left, Center, Far-right)

UI: python3 ui.py opens a browser page at http://127.0.0.1:8000 with a form (parties, voters, seed, sperregrense on/off, mandate split) and a per-district table. ui.py only renders HTML and calls functions from election.py; keep all voting logic in election.py.
